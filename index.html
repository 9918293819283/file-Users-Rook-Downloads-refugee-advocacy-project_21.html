A student-built initiative

What would you carry, if you had ten minutes to leave everything behind?

We're a group of students turning a single classroom activity into something any school, library, or living room can run — so more people understand what it actually means to become a refugee.

See the activity Get involved
Why this exists

Most people know refugees exist. Few have had to imagine packing one bag for the rest of their life.

This project started as a classroom activity at Kealing Middle School in 2023, where students had minutes to decide what a family would take if they were leaving home and never coming back. This meant deciding what was left behind. This hands-on activity required 6th graders to work together to make quick decisions and gain some understanding about the refugee process. We think this issue deserves a wider audience.

We are rebuilding this project as something that isn't locked to one classroom: a toolkit that any teacher, librarian, or community group can pick up and run themselves, alongside public events that put awareness in front of a community that doesn't always get the chance to sit with this.

The toolkit

Here's what's going in the bag

We're building this as a facilitator-ready kit, sourced in part from UNHCR's own resources, so the exercise carries the same weight wherever it's run.

ITEM 01

Facilitator guide

Everything a teacher or group leader needs to run the activity cold — timing, discussion prompts, and debrief questions.

ITEM 02

What's in My Bag

Participants have minutes to choose what fits in a single bag before they have to leave. What they keep, and what they can't, is the whole lesson.

ITEM 03

Stay or Go

A set of scenario cards where participants weigh real decisions displaced families face — with no clean answer to any of them.

ITEM 04

Fact sheet & sources

Plain-language context on who refugees are and aren't, pulled from UNHCR and other primary sources — built to correct common myths.

Item 03 — facilitator guide

Running the full session

Suggested agenda (60–75 min)

  • Intro & framing — 5 min
  • What's in My Bag — 25–35 min, including debrief
  • Short break — 5 min
  • Stay or Go — 25–35 min, including debrief
  • Fact sheet & closing — 10 min

Room setup checklist

  • A visible timer or countdown (built into Item 01)
  • Two clear zones of the room, labeled STAY and GO
  • Index cards if running the physical version of What's in My Bag
  • A projector or shared screen, if running the digital activities as a class

Facilitation principles

  • Keep the framing serious. These activities work because the pressure is real, not performed.
  • Don't rush the debrief. The silence after a hard question is doing work — let it sit.
  • Give a quiet opt-out. Some participants may have a personal connection to displacement; let them observe instead of participate, without singling them out.
  • Close every session with the fact sheet, so the emotional weight of the activity lands on real information, not just a feeling.
  • Preview The Journey before running it with a group. Some paths include a family member not surviving the trip — a real and common part of displacement, but worth knowing about in advance so you can decide how to introduce it.

Item 04 — fact sheet

Myths and facts

MYTH

Most refugees resettle in wealthy Western countries.

FACT

The large majority are hosted by neighboring low- and middle-income countries, not by wealthy nations far away.

MYTH

Refugees choose to leave for economic opportunity, like other migrants.

FACT

Refugees are fleeing persecution, conflict, or violence, and are protected under international law in a category distinct from economic migration.

MYTH

Seeking asylum is a quick or automatic process.

FACT

Asylum and resettlement processes often take years, with rigorous vetting at every stage.

MYTH

Once someone is a refugee, that status is permanent.

FACT

Most refugees hope to return home, and many do when conditions allow — though displacement often lasts far longer than anyone expects at the start.

What people actually carry

Across recent crises, the same handful of items shows up again and again — alongside whatever else a family can't bring itself to leave behind.

  • Ukraine: money, passports, identification papers, food
  • South Sudan: money, passports, identification papers, food
  • Afghanistan: money, passports, identification papers, food, a Quran
  • DRC: money, passports, identification papers, food

Sources for further reading

What's in My Bag 1 / 7
Item 01 · Facilitator-led

What's in My Bag

A timed-decision simulation on displacement  ·  Group size: 15–40  ·  Time: 25–35 min  ·  Age: middle school and up

This is a live-run activity, meant to be projected or read aloud. Step through each slide with your class — the timers are built in.

Before you begin

Setup

Give each participant (or small group of 2–3) an index card listing household items — mix the mundane and the precious:

  • Passport / ID papers
  • Phone + charger
  • Universal adapter/converter
  • Family photo album
  • School records/diplomas/transcript
  • Cash
  • Credit cards
  • Prescription medication
  • Matches/lighter
  • Book(s)
  • Stuffed animal
  • Small toys
  • Winter coat
  • Laptop
  • Family heirlooms (small silver photo frame, military medals, plaques)
  • Small jewelry box (wedding rings, gold and silver necklaces, bracelets, rings, earrings)
  • Box of documents (car and/or house deed, lease agreements, birth certificates, employment letters, paystubs, passwords)
  • First aid kit (bandages, ointment, antiseptic spray, pain medicine)
  • A religious or cultural object
  • Water bottle
  • Water filter
  • Towel
  • Bed sheets
  • Small laundry detergent
  • Blanket
  • Stroller
  • Baby sling
  • Hammock
  • A change of clothes per family member
  • Personal hygiene kit (soap, shampoo, toothbrushes and toothpaste)
  • Address book
  • Non-perishable food
  • Small pot and pan
  • Kitchen utensils (one set of knife, fork and spoon)
  • Ladle/serving spoon
  • Toilet paper
  • Can opener
  • Disposable plates, napkins and cups
  • Inflatable pool ring
  • A musical instrument
  • A diary or personal journal
  • Old letters/postcards
  • A toolset (hammer, rope, screwdriver, nails)
  • Batteries
  • Flashlight

One bag per person or group — real objects and props, or a taped outline the size of a school backpack. The size should visibly fit only 6–8 items, not all of them.

Round 1 · read this aloud, don't paraphrase

4 a.m.

"It's 4 a.m. Someone is pounding on your door. A neighbor is shouting that the checkpoint down the road has fallen and the road out of the city will close by morning. You will not be coming back — not this week, not maybe ever.

You have one bag. Whatever doesn't fit, stays. You have 90 seconds. Go."
Round 1 — pack the bag
01:30
"Bags down. Hands off."
Round 2 · the twist — don't skip this

New information

"The road you were planning to take is a mountain path — no vehicles. Everything you're carrying, you carry on your back for three days. Look at your bag. You have 30 seconds to drop down to 5 items."
Round 2 — cut down to 5
00:30
"Bags down. Hands off."

Round 1 is about instinct. Round 2 is about triage — that's the actual lesson.

Debrief · 10–15 min — ask in order, don't rush past silence

Talk it through

  1. "What's the first thing you grabbed, before you even thought?"
  2. "What did you put in, then take back out in round 2? Why that one?"
  3. "Was there anything you wanted to bring that wasn't on the card at all?"
  4. "Who did you make this decision for — just yourself, or other people too?"
  5. "Real families don't get a card with 20 options pre-picked for them, and they don't get a do-over. What does that change about how you'd choose?"
While you debrief

Facilitator notes

  • Don't let round 1 become a game to "win" by being clever. There isn't a best answer.
  • Watch for any students who go quiet. This might be hard for kids with any personal or family connection to displacement. We have found that the students who have experience with displacement understand this activity better than the rest of the students, and they have been eager to take charge of the activity. However, if there is a student who feels this activity is too emotionally difficult, have the student engage in alternate activities such as journaling or quiet reflection.
  • Have tissues visible but don't announce them.
Close

One fact, stated plainly

Most displaced people leave with what they can carry in minutes, expecting to return within weeks — and the median length of displacement worldwide is well over a decade.

Stay or Go 1 / 8
Item 02 · Facilitator-led

Stay or Go

A scenario-based discussion on displacement decisions  ·  Group size: 10–40  ·  Time: 25–35 min  ·  Age: middle school and up

Four scenarios, each with a hidden complication. Participants take a side before hearing the whole story — and usually find out there wasn't a clean answer to begin with.

Before you begin

Setup

  • Label two sides of the room STAY and GO. Participants can physically move to a side, or you can run it as a show of hands and use the vote counters on each slide to tally.
  • Read each scenario aloud, let the group commit to a side, then reveal the complication — not before.
  • There is no correct answer in any scenario. Resist telling students which choice was "right."
Scenario 1

The Last Checkpoint

You've reached the final checkpoint before the border closes for the night. The guard says only people holding valid papers may cross tonight. Yours are valid. Your grandmother's were destroyed when soldiers searched your home last month, and there's no time to replace them before curfew.

Do you cross without her, hoping to bring her later — or stay together, and risk the border closing for good?

Where does the room stand?
Families who split up at a checkpoint like this often aren't reunited for years, if ever — but families who stayed together sometimes found the border sealed permanently within days.
Scenario 2

The Camp Choice

You've been in a refugee camp with your two children for eight months. A caseworker says an official transfer to permanent housing in a safe country could take two to five years of waiting. A cousin offers to lend you money for a guide who says he can get your family across the nearest border unofficially — tonight.

Do you wait for the slow, legal path — or go now, off the official record?

Where does the room stand?
The legal path is safer, but the wait can outlast a childhood. The unofficial route is faster, but has no protection if anything goes wrong along the way — for the family, or for the guide.
Scenario 3

An Ocean Between

Two years after fleeing, you've rebuilt something stable in a neighboring country — steady work, and your kids in school near their aunt and cousins, the only family you have left nearby. Then you're offered a resettlement slot in a country far away, with real opportunity for your children's future, but no one you know there.

Do you stay near the family you have left — or go, for a future they might not otherwise get?

Where does the room stand?
Every year, some refugees turn down resettlement offers for exactly this reason — because no version of "better" exists that doesn't also mean leaving the last people who remember you from before.
Scenario 4

When the Fighting Stops

Three years after leaving, you hear the fighting near your old neighborhood has ended. Reports say your street is still standing, though power and water aren't reliable yet. Meanwhile, your children have just started to feel at home here — new friends, a school they like.

Do you go home, to the place that's still yours — or stay, in the life your family has only just begun to rebuild?

Where does the room stand?
Ceasefires don't always hold, and "still standing" doesn't mean "safe to live in" — but many refugees say the hardest part of staying isn't the new country. It's the guilt of not going back.
Debrief · 10–15 min — ask in order, don't rush past silence

Talk it through

  1. "Which scenario was hardest to decide? What made it hard?"
  2. "Did anyone change their answer after hearing the complication? What changed your mind?"
  3. "Was there ever a 'right' answer in any of these — or just trade-offs?"
  4. "How did it feel to make one of these decisions for other people — a grandmother, a child — not just yourself?"
  5. "These aren't hypothetical for millions of people. What would you want someone actually facing one of these to know?"
Close

No version of this loses nothing

Every scenario had a cost on both sides. That's not a flaw in the activity — it's the actual shape of the decision, for real families making it right now.
